In *Brave New World* (1932), John the Savage grows up in Malpais, a pueblo on a New Mexico
reservation, and now and then speaks "the Indian words". Readers have long taken them for Zuni,
and the novel says so twice: when he curses through a locked door, "It was only in Zuñi that the
Savage could adequately express what he felt" (chapter 12), and in his hermitage he prays "now in
Zuñi to Awonawilona" (chapter 18). This note asks three questions. Which words are they, and where
did Huxley find them? What do they mean? And what kind of language is Zuni? The answer to the
first is narrower than expected: nearly every word traces to one book, Frank Hamilton Cushing's
*Zuñi Folk Tales* (1901), and the most famous of them is not an insult at all.

Huxley wrote the novel in the south of France between May and August 1931. Cushing lived at Zuni from 1879 to 1884 and was initiated into its Priesthood of
the Bow in 1881; his tales were taken down from Zuni narrators in the 1880s and published in 1901,
the year after his death. Quotations from the novel follow the first edition's spellings, with their accents.

## One page behind the curse

The strongest evidence is a single page. Cushing introduces one tale, "How the Twins of War and
Chance, Áhaiyúta and Mátsailéma, Fared with the Unborn-Made Men of the Underworld", with a scene
at his own fireside. He has asked his storyteller Waíhusiwa, "of the priestly kin of Zuñi", for
"only one more story of the grandfathers". Waíhusiwa claims his "word-pouch is as empty as the
food-pack of a lost hunter", until "old Pálowahtiwa, who was sitting near" tells him to feel in
the bottom of it and tell of the Underworld. Waíhusiwa begins with a question, Cushing answers in
the listener's formula, and the narrator cuts him off:

> "Nay, never; not even from my own grandfathers," said I. "Sons éso to your story; short be it
> or long."
>
> "Sons éso tse-ná!" ("Cool your 'sons éso!' and wait till I begin.")
>
> Cushing, *Zuñi Folk Tales*, p. 399

A footnote on the same page explains the formula it mocks. A Zuni tale opened with the teller's
"Són ah-tchi!" ("Let us take up"), answered by the listeners' "É-so!" ("Yea, verily"); after the
teller's next line the listeners said "Sons éso! Te-ä-tú!" ("Yea, let us, verily! Be it so").
*Sons éso* is the audience's "yes, go on". Waíhusiwa's *tse-ná* turns it back on an eager
listener who has said it too soon: hold your "yes, go on" until there is a story.

Three things tie Huxley to this page and not merely to Zuni in general. The phrase is spelled
exactly as Cushing spells it, accents included. The two names on the page, Waíhusiwa and
Pálowahtiwa, both turn up in Malpais in chapter 7: Palowhtiwa is the youth who "only got as far as
seven" in the whipping ritual, and "poor Waihusiwa" is one of Linda's lovers, whom John as a boy
once tried to kill. And the tale's title
gives Huxley his description of the war gods: "Ahaiyuta and Marsailema, the twins of War and
Chance" (chapter 8), with Cushing's *Mátsailéma* turned into *Marsailema*. The other tales of the
same book account for the rest of the table: Mitsima's magic, the word *háni*, the girl's and the
boy's names. A reader of Cushing's book, and of no other Zuni text, could have written every Zuni
word in the novel except one.

## What the words mean

Huxley presents the words as obscenities. When John refuses to meet the Arch-Community-Songster,
"it was lucky that Bernard didn't understand Zuñi" (chapter 12), and he says the same five words to
the reporter he then kicks (chapter 18). Read against Cushing, they say something milder and
stranger.

**"Háni!"** In Cushing's tale of the cannibal demon Átahsaia, two sisters live together: "the
elder sister called to the younger, 'Háni!' 'What sayest thou?' said the háni." In the tale of the
Maiden of Mátsaki the witch sister envies "the smile on her hani's face", the face of "her younger
sister". In Cushing's usage, then, *háni* is a kin term, the word for a younger sister and the
name an older sister calls her by. Shouted at Bernard it would be something like "little sister!",
a putdown perhaps, but not the curse the narrator implies. Cushing glosses it only by context;
Huxley does not gloss it at all.

**"Sons éso tse-ná!"** As Cushing glosses it in the passage above, it is a teller's retort to
an impatient listener. Shouted through a door at Bernard, who has come to fetch John for the
Arch-Songster, it carries an accidental sense: wait, there is nothing for you yet. Nothing in
Cushing marks it as an insult or a fixed phrase; he prints it as one narrator's reply on one
evening, built out of the listeners' formula.

**Mitsima's magic.** In the tale of the Maiden of Mátsaki, a squirrel and his wife plant a seed and
dance around it to make a hemlock grow tall enough to rescue a young man stranded on a cliff:

> "Kiäthlä tsilu, / Silokwe, silokwe, silokwe; / Ki'ai silu silu, / Tsithl! Tsithl!"
>
> Which meant, as far as any one can tell now (for it was a long time ago, and partly squirrel
> talk), "Hemlock of the / Tall kind, tall kind, tall kind, / Sprout up hemlock, hemlock, / Chit!
> Chit!"
>
> Cushing, *Zuñi Folk Tales*, pp. 27–28

Huxley keeps the words and drops the diaeresis, the apostrophe and the exclamation marks. Cushing
himself hedges the meaning ("as far as any one can tell now"), and the last word is the squirrel's
chatter.

**"Ai yaa tákwa!"** This one is not in Cushing's tales. "Ai yaaaa!" is there, as the cry of the
ugly wild boy who drives the bear away (p. 314), and the novel has old Mitsima sing "a song about
killing a bear" (chapter 8), so the exclamation may come from that tale. No source for *tákwa*
could be found. An unpublished Utrecht thesis (van Rij, 2006), known here only from a reader's
comment that quotes it, reports that *tákwa* is not in Newman's Zuni dictionary of 1958 and that a
Zuni tourism officer, asked in 2006, took it to mean "one who does not know anything". That is one
person's guess at a word Huxley may have misspelled or invented, and it should be read as such.

Cushing's glosses are a translator's, and a contested one. Members of the Pueblo have objected that
he documented the Zuni way of life wrongfully, publishing what was sacred, and the Zuni once tried
him for taking sacred objects. His versions are fluent, archaizing English, and none of the glosses above has been checked here against a
modern Zuni dictionary.

## A Zuni language in a composite reservation

The language in the novel is Zuni, but the world around it is assembled from several peoples.

- **Pookong**, to whom John prays beside Jesus, is Hopi. H. R. Voth's *The Traditions of the Hopi*
  (1905) tells of Pöokónghoya and his brother Balö'onghoya, who live with their grandmother Spider
  Woman; Huxley's "Pookong" is that name without its suffix.
- **Etsanatlehi**, "the woman who makes herself young again", is Navajo: Changing Woman, whom
  Washington Matthews spells Estsánatlehi. The gloss is Huxley's own.
- **Popé**, Linda's lover, bears the name of the Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who led
  the Pueblo Revolt against Spanish rule in 1680.
- **Our Lady of Acoma** and **the Black Stone at Laguna** belong to Acoma and Laguna, Keresan-
  speaking pueblos east of Zuni; the flagellant **Penitentes** of chapter 11 are a Hispanic
  New Mexican brotherhood.

The cosmology John learns in chapter 8 shows how Huxley handled his sources. Cushing's "Outlines of
Zuñi Creation Myths" (1896) begins: "Awonawilona conceived within himself and thought outward in
space, whereby mists of increase, steams potent of growth, were evolved and uplifted", and life was
conceived "in the Fourfold womb of the World". Huxley's old men tell it as "Awonawilona, who made a
great fog by thinking in the night, and then made the whole world out of the fog", and John's
reading primer has "the Fog of Increase" and "four wombs". The phrases are compressed, not
invented. So the reservation is a collage of the Southwest's peoples, drawn from the ethnography
of Huxley's day, but the language, where the novel names it, is consistently Zuni, and is consistently
Cushing's.

## Why half-understood words

Huxley's use of Zuni connects to the novel's larger argument about words. John meets the old men's
stories "in the other words", and they are "all the more wonderful to him for being told in the
other words and so not fully understood" (chapter 8). When he first reads Shakespeare, the lines
roll through his mind "like old Mitsima saying magic over his feathers and his carved sticks and
his bits of bone and stone" (Huxley then prints the magic itself, "kiathla tsilu silokwe
silokwe silokwe"), but "better than Mitsima's
magic, because it meant more". Both languages work on John as incantation, sound first and sense
second, and the novel rests on the contrast: Shakespeare gives him words to hate with, while the
Zuni words stand for a world he belongs to only in part.

Read against Cushing, the incantation is a squirrel's song for making a tree grow, and the curse
is a narrator's teasing at a fireside. Huxley did not need the meanings, only the sound of a real
language that his readers, like Bernard, could not follow. The result is that the Savage's curse is,
by its only source, an invitation to be patient.

## Zuni, the language

Zuni (*Shiwi'ma*, "the Zuni way") is spoken at the Pueblo of Zuni in western New Mexico. Its
speakers call themselves *A꞉shiwi*, the plural of *shiwi*, "Zuni"; the English name is thought to
come from the Acoma (Keresan) word *sɨ̂‧ni*.

**Sounds.** Zuni has five vowels, each short or long, and sixteen consonants, among them a glottal
stop, a labialized *kʷ*, and a voiceless lateral fricative, *ɬ*, the sound of Welsh *ll*. The current
orthography writes it *ł*, Stanley Newman's grammar *lh*, and Cushing *thl*. That is why Huxley's
Zuni is full of *thl* (Kothlu, tsithl, kiathla): he copied a nineteenth-century spelling of one
consonant. Cushing's word for folk tales, *té-la-p'-na-we*, appears in the modern orthography as
*delapna꞉we*, the storytelling register.

**Grammar.** Word order is fairly free, tending to subject–object–verb. Nouns carry no case, but
there are three numbers, singular, dual and plural, and the pronouns have subject, object and
possessive forms for the first and second persons only: Zuni has no third-person pronoun. Verbs are
long and complex, and can take in a noun loosely ("noun incorporation"); a verb can also mark
whether the next clause has the same subject or a different one (switch-reference). Besides the
everyday language there are a storytelling register, a ceremonial one with vocabulary of its
own, and slang; Newman described the sacred and slang levels in 1955.

**Neighbours.** Zuni shares features with Hopi, the Keresan languages and the Tanoan languages,
and to a lesser degree with Navajo: ejective consonants, final devoicing, a dual number and a
ceremonial vocabulary. Campbell (1997) treats these as the marks of a linguistic area, the result
of centuries of contact, not of common descent, and Zuni's religious vocabulary includes words
borrowed from Keresan, Hopi and O'odham.

## An isolate, and the Penutian proposal

Zuni is classified as a language isolate: no relationship to any other language has been shown.
Several have been proposed.

- **Penutian.** Stanley Newman, the language's main grammarian, published "Comparison of Zuni and
  California Penutian" in 1964, the most fully argued proposal. Newman himself, according to
  Michael Silverstein as reported by Ives Goddard (1996), regarded it as a tongue-in-cheek piece.
  Campbell (1997) finds the usual faults in its cognate sets: borrowed words (*tobacco*), loose
  meanings (*bad* compared with *garbage*, *horse* with *hoof*), nursery words and onomatopoeia.
  Wikipedia's article on Penutian, citing Jane Hill (2002, 2010), calls the link generally
  rejected and possibly intended as a hoax.
- **Larger groupings.** Edward Sapir put Zuni in his "Aztec-Tanoan" in 1929, without supporting
  evidence; Morris Swadesh's "Penutioid" and Joseph Greenberg's Penutian included it without
  convincing argument, in Campbell's judgment.
- **Neighbours.** Zuni has been linked to Keresan and to Tanoan, and J. P. Harrington wrote an
  unpublished paper titled "Zuñi Discovered to be Hokan". None of these is accepted.

What would change the classification is the kind of evidence Newman's lists lacked: regular sound
correspondences across basic vocabulary, of the kind that ties Hopi to the rest of Uto-Aztecan,
rather than resemblances that contact or chance can produce. With neighbours as close and as
long-standing as Zuni's, loans are the main trap, and the shared features above are the reason
the burden of proof is high.

The positions in this section are known here from secondary accounts: Wikipedia's articles on Zuni
and on Penutian, which cite Campbell, Goddard and Hill, and the Crossref records of Newman's and Hill's papers.
None of the works was opened in full.

## History and status

*As of November 2026.*

The Zuni have lived in the Zuni River valley for many centuries. Spanish contact came in 1539, when
Esteban, an enslaved Moor in Fray Marcos de Niza's party, reached Hawikuh, which Niza then claimed
as one of the Seven Cities of Cíbola. Coronado's army fought six hundred Zuni warriors near Hawikuh
in July 1540 and took the village. Before the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 the Zuni lived in six villages;
after it they took refuge on the mesa of Dowa Yalanne ("corn mountain") until 1692. The
reservation was set up in 1877, two years before Cushing arrived.

Zuni is still spoken across the pueblo, but it is losing ground to English among children. Wikipedia gives about 9,500 speakers, without a source;
an older Ethnologue entry gives 9,650 for 2000, of whom the 2000 census counted 7,010 who used the
language at home. Glottolog's endangerment entry rates Zuni "shifting" (Campbell et al., 2022)
and "threatened" (Golla et al., 2008). Wikipedia, citing the Pueblo of Zuni's Head Start report for
2018, says 16 percent of the enrolled children spoke Zuni; the counts it quotes, 15 children with
Zuni as their first language and 137 with English, come to 10 percent, so the share is somewhere
in that range depending on how bilingual children were counted. The current orthography was
worked out largely by Curtis Cook, and the tribe has worked with the public school district,
founded in 1980, on a literacy programme in it.

## Where the evidence is thin

- **What Huxley read.** No letter or diary of Huxley's naming Cushing was found. The case rests on
  the words themselves: exact spellings, the two names on the same page as the curse, and the
  borrowed title "Twins of War and Chance". An open casebook on the novel (Sexton and Soles) states
  that Huxley used Cushing's Smithsonian reports and read Walter Hough's pamphlet *The Moki Snake
  Dance* in 1930, but gives no source for either claim. The creation myth could also have reached
  him through Stith Thompson's anthology (1929), which reprints that passage, but the curse, the
  squirrel's song and the names are not in it.
- **"Ai yaa tákwa".** *Tákwa* is unlocated; the gloss above is secondhand and uncertain.
- **The glosses.** Every meaning in this note is Cushing's, from the 1880s. Cushing was a Bow
  priest and spoke Zuni, but he wrote literary English versions, and his authority among the Zuni
  is itself disputed. A Zuni speaker, or Newman's dictionary, could correct any of them.
- **Status figures.** The speaker counts are old or unsourced, and the Head Start figure is quoted
  through Wikipedia, whose own numbers don't match its percentage.
