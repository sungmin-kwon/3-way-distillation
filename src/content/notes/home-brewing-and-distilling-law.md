distillate: In most countries I checked, brewing beer or making wine at home for your own use is legal and untaxed, while distilling the same wash into spirits needs a commercial licence that hobbyists can't get. The law treats the two halves of making spirits very differently. Fermenting a sugary liquid into beer, wine or cider at home is allowed, within limits, in most of the countries below. Boiling that same liquid in a still, which is all a [pot still](note:how-spirits-distillation-works) does, is a licensed industrial activity almost everywhere. This note collects the rules I could check against primary sources, country by country, and says where I couldn't.

> **This is not legal advice.** It is a reading of statutes and official guidance **as of 10 November 2026**, with links so you can check them yourself. Laws and their enforcement change, regional and local rules can be stricter than national ones, and the US position in particular may change when the Supreme Court decides. Anything I couldn't confirm from a primary source is marked as such.

Throughout, I'll use one worked example, the stripping run from the [spirits note](note:how-spirits-distillation-works):

| | value |
| --- | --- |
| wash | 20 L at 8% ABV, fermented at home |
| ethanol in the wash | 20 L × 0.08 = 1.6 L |
| still | a small pot still of the kind sold online |
| purpose | own use, nothing sold or given away |

The 1.6 L of ethanol would make about 4 L of spirit at 40% ABV if all of it were recovered. The question for each country is which of three steps is legal: fermenting the wash, owning the still, and running the wash through it.

## Why brewing and distilling are treated differently

The main reason is tax. Spirits concentrate alcohol, so a small, easily hidden volume carries a lot of duty, and most excise systems are built around licensing every place that produces it. Several of the provisions below are tax law: the UK rules sit in a Finance Act, the Australian ones in the Excise Act, and the Japanese and German ones in liquor and alcohol tax acts. The US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit made the same point from the other side in April 2026, calling the home-distilling ban "anti-revenue" because it stops production entirely instead of taxing it.[^mcnutt]

Safety is the usual second reason, and it is weaker than the common story. The fear is methanol, but methanol isn't concentrated in the heads of a run; it comes over across the whole run, and the amount in a spirit is set mostly by the fruit and the fermentation, as the [spirits note](note:how-spirits-distillation-works) explains. A grain or sugar wash like the example contains very little. The real home hazards are a still running dry or leaking hot ethanol vapor near a flame, and drinking the foreshots. None of the laws below distinguish safe and unsafe practice; they distinguish licensed from unlicensed.

## The rules, country by country

A summary for the worked example. "Brew" means fermenting the wash for own use; "distill" means running it through the still at home without a commercial licence.

| country | brew at home | own a small still | distill at home | main source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | yes, up to 100 or 200 US gal a year | registration rules apply[^stills-us] | federally banned; ban held unconstitutional in the Fifth Circuit, now at the Supreme Court | 26 U.S.C. 5053(e), 5042(a)(2), 5178, 5601 |
| United Kingdom | yes, for own domestic use | not confirmed | no, needs HMRC approval | Finance (No. 2) Act 2023 ss. 72, 82, 84 |
| Canada | wine yes; beer not checked | not confirmed | no, needs a spirits licence | Excise Act, 2001 ss. 60, 62 |
| Australia | yes, non-commercial | up to 5 L without permission, but not for alcohol | no, no licences for personal use | Excise Act 1901; ATO guidance |
| New Zealand | yes | yes | **yes**, for personal use, not for sale | Customs and Excise Act 2018 s. 67(4)(b) |
| Germany | yes, 5 hL of beer a year tax-free | over 2 L must be reported | no, since 1 January 2018 | zoll.de guidance |
| Sweden | yes, for own use | no, without permission | no | Alkohollag (2010:1622) ch. 2 |
| Japan | no, not at 1% ABV or more | not checked | no | Liquor Tax Act arts. 7, 54 |
| Hungary | not checked | not checked | was legal from 2010; current rules not confirmed | CJEU C-115/13 (2014) |

### United States

**Brewing and winemaking are legal federally.** Any adult may produce beer "for personal or family use and not for sale" without tax, up to 100 US gallons a calendar year in a household with one adult and 200 gallons with two or more.[^us-beer] Wine has the same limits under 26 U.S.C. 5042(a)(2). The beer regulation adds that the exemption "does not authorize the production of beer for use contrary to State or local law," so state and local rules still apply. The beer exemption dates from a 1978 law that took effect in 1979; before that, home brewing was federally illegal even though home winemaking was not. Our 20 L wash is a little over 5 US gallons, far inside either limit.

**Distilling at home is federally banned, for now.** TTB's guidance, last updated March 2025, says that "Federal law strictly prohibits individuals from producing distilled spirits at home," and its penalties page cites 26 U.S.C. 5601, including distilling on prohibited premises, with up to 5 years in prison and a $10,000 fine per offense.[^ttb] The prohibited-premises rule is 26 U.S.C. 5178(a)(1)(B), which bars a distilled spirits plant in "any dwelling house" or a shed, yard or enclosure connected to one. That is the provision behind the reports of a "158-year-old ban," since it goes back to 1868. There is no hobby permit; TTB permits are for commercial distilled spirits plants.

**The courts are now split.** The Hobby Distillers Association and individual hobbyists sued in the Northern District of Texas in December 2023 (*McNutt v. DOJ*). On 10 April 2026 the Fifth Circuit held that the ban exceeded Congress's power under the Taxation Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause, and it denied the government's request for rehearing in June.[^mcnutt] Eleven days after the Fifth Circuit ruled, the Sixth Circuit upheld the same provisions in a separate case, *Ream v. U.S. Department of the Treasury*.[^husch] The government petitioned the Supreme Court, which **granted certiorari in McNutt on 19 October 2026**; as of this writing it has not set an argument date, and the Ream petition was neither granted nor denied.[^scotus]

**What that means in practice is narrow.** The ruling removes the *location* barrier, not the need for a federal permit, federal regulation, excise tax, and state and local law. According to the Hobby Distillers Association, since July 2026 TTB has been asking applicants whose home location would otherwise be disqualifying whether they are association members, and approving some such permits with a notice that the government is still seeking further review.[^hda-ttb] Sources disagree on how far the ruling reaches: one law firm describes the ban as unenforceable in Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, while the association has said the ruling applies only to the plaintiffs, which includes its members.[^husch] [^hda-ttb] Treat the scope as unsettled. For the worked example, running the wash through the still at home without a federal permit remains a federal offense outside that narrow group, and a Supreme Court reversal would end even that.

### United Kingdom

UK alcohol duty was rewritten by the Finance (No. 2) Act 2023, which repealed the Alcoholic Liquor Duties Act 1979 from 1 August 2023. Older guides that cite the 1979 Act, including its distiller's licence, are out of date.[^uk-alda]

- **Brewing is legal.** Section 82 says a person may not produce alcoholic products without approval from HMRC, but section 84 exempts a person who "produces alcoholic products only for the person's own domestic use" where "the alcoholic products are not spirits." Section 72 exempts the same products from duty. There is no volume limit in the text.[^uk-fa]
- **Distilling is not.** Spirits are expressly excluded from both exemptions, so making spirits needs an alcoholic products producer approval. HMRC's guidance is written for commercial producers and offers no hobby route.[^uk-guide]
- **Stills: not confirmed.** The 1979 Act had separate rules on making and possessing stills. I didn't find a still provision in the parts of the 2023 Act I read, and I haven't confirmed what replaced the old rules, so I'm not stating one.

The wash is legal; the spirit run is not.

### Canada

Under the federal Excise Act, 2001, nobody may produce wine except under a wine licence, but section 62(2)(a) exempts "the production of wine by an individual for their personal use," and section 63 bars selling it.[^ca-act] The Canada Revenue Agency says this lets individuals make wine at home or at a ferment-on-premises shop for "personal, non-commercial use."[^ca-edm] Spirits are different: section 60 requires a spirits licence, and the CRA's list of exceptions has no personal-use one. Beer is taxed under the older Excise Act of 1985, which I didn't check, and provinces add their own rules, so I've marked beer as unchecked.

### Australia

The Australian Taxation Office is unusually direct. Home-brewed beer, cider and wine "can be made without an excise manufacturer licence" when made for non-commercial purposes with non-commercial equipment. For spirits: "To use a still of any capacity to distil alcohol, you must have an excise manufacturer licence," and "We don't grant licences to distil spirits for personal consumption."[^ato] You may own a still of 5 L or less without permission "only if you're not using it to distil alcohol"; a larger one needs ATO permission even to possess. Duty is "generally payable on alcohol you distil, even if you don't have a licence," with penalties up to five times the duty or prosecution.

So the wash is legal, the 5 L still is legal to own, and the spirit run is illegal at any still size.

### New Zealand

New Zealand is the clearest case where home distilling is legal. Section 67(4)(b) of the Customs and Excise Act 2018 exempts "the manufacture by an individual of beer, wine, or spirits in his or her private dwelling" if made "exclusively for the individual's personal use and not for sale or other disposition to any other person."[^nz] The text sets no volume limit. The same exemption was in section 68B of the Customs and Excise Act 1996, inserted in 2009; the legalization is usually dated to 1996, but I could only check the 2009 text.

The whole worked example is legal, provided the 4 L of spirit is drunk by you and not sold or given away. "Other disposition to any other person" is broad, so even a gift is outside the exemption on a plain reading.

### Germany

German customs (Zoll) allows home and hobby brewers to brew up to **5 hectolitres of beer per calendar year** tax-free, solely for their own consumption and not for sale.[^de-beer] Distilling is closed: since 1 January 2018, distillation of alcohol is allowed only in bonded or licensed small distilleries (*Verschluss-* or *Abfindungsbrennereien*), and the old exception for small stills of up to 0.5 L for private use "no longer exists." Zoll's conclusion is that private distillation "is therefore not permitted."[^de-alc] Stills over 2 L must be reported to the main customs office. The lawful route for a private person is to become a *Stoffbesitzer*, someone with their own fruit who has it distilled under permit at a licensed distillery.

### Sweden

Sweden's Alcohol Act, chapter 2, allows only approved bonded manufacturers to make spirits, with no private exemption, and the same licensing rule for wine and beer "does not apply to manufacture at home for own use."[^se] Sweden goes further than most on equipment: an apparatus "obviously suited" to making spirits may only be made for, transferred to or possessed by someone entitled to make spirits, unless the Public Health Agency (*Folkhälsomyndigheten*) grants an exception. In the worked example, owning the still is itself an offense.

### Japan

Japan is the strict case for brewing too. The Liquor Tax Act defines liquor as a drink of **1% ABV or more**, and making it requires a licence from the tax office, with penalties of up to 10 years' imprisonment or a ¥1 million fine.[^jp-brew] A beer kit is legal only if the result stays under 1%, and the National Tax Agency notes that a beer licence isn't available below 60 kL a year. The one well-known allowance is infusion: for your own drinking, adding fruit to tax-paid liquor of 20% ABV or more, as for *umeshu*, isn't treated as manufacture, but not with grains, grapes and several other additives, and the result can't be sold.[^jp-umeshu] Both the wash and the spirit run are illegal.

### Hungary, and the EU limit

Hungary shows how EU excise law limits national choices. From 27 September 2010 Hungary let households distill fruit spirit (*pálinka*) at home, with an annual duty-free allowance; reports give 50 L at 86% ABV.[^hu-2010] On 10 April 2014 the Court of Justice of the EU held in *Commission v Hungary* (C-115/13) that this breached the EU excise directive, which allows at most a reduced rate, not full exemption, on a limited quantity distilled for fruit growers' households, and has no exception at all for private home distillation.[^cjeu] Hungary changed its rules after that ruling, but I couldn't confirm the current limits, tax rate or registration requirement from a primary source, so I'm leaving them out.

## Not covered, or not confirmed

I left out countries where I couldn't reach a primary source this time. The ones I'd add next:

- **Ireland**, where illicit distilling (*poitín*) has a long legal history; I couldn't confirm the current statute or the status of home brewing.
- **South Korea**, which is often said to have legalized home brewing for personal use in 1995; I couldn't read the Liquor Tax Act text.
- **Russia**, where home distilling for personal use is often said to be legal; not confirmed.
- **US states**, which have their own rules on top of federal law. The Hobby Distillers Association counts "only a handful" that allow home distilling for personal use, and state permission has never overridden the federal ban.
- **Countries that prohibit alcohol outright**, where both halves are illegal.

## Summary

Fermenting a wash for your own use is legal in most of the countries checked here, Japan being the strict exception. Distilling it at home is not, except in New Zealand and, for a narrow and contested group, the United States. The split comes from how spirits are taxed rather than from any special danger in distilling, and it moves: Germany closed its small-still exception in 2018, the EU court closed Hungary's in 2014, and the US Supreme Court may settle the American question once it hears *McNutt*. Check the current law, and the local law, before buying a still.
