Wei never unified China. Its general Sima Yan founded Jin in 265, and Jin took the surrender of
Wu in 280, sixty years after the Han emperor abdicated to Cao Pi. Readers of the period still
argue over when that ending became settled. One answer is the battle of Guandu (官渡) in 200,
where Cao Cao beat Yuan Shao and won the north. Another holds that Chibi (赤壁) in 208 reopened
the game, and that Shu and Wu kept a real chance until Liu Bei's defeat at Yiling (夷陵) in
222; others put the end of that chance at the loss of Jingzhou in 219. This note takes a position from the sources: the two answers are about different
questions, and the date that best fits "after this, the north wins" is neither 200 nor 219
nor 222 but 263.

The sources are Chen Shou's *Records of the Three Kingdoms* (三國志, finished around 290),
Pei Songzhi's commentary on it (429), which quotes many works now lost, the *Book of Jin* (晉書)
and Du You's *Comprehensive Institutions* (通典, 801). These works are divided into volumes (卷),
literally "scrolls": a volume began as one roll of text and is closer to a long chapter than to
a modern book, so citations below give the volume number ("vol. 7"). Quotations are given in the original
with a translation; translations are our own. A complete Korean translation of Chen Shou with
all of Pei's commentary, by Kim Yeong-mun (김영문), appeared in 2026 (Lee, 2026).

## The registers

Throughout, the note returns to one set of numbers: the households (戶) and persons (口) that
each state had on its registers, as Du You collected them in *Comprehensive Institutions*, vol. 7. They are the
nearest thing the period has to a measure of strength.

| Register | Households | Persons | Persons per household |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eastern Han, 157 | 10,677,960 | 56,486,856 | 5.29 |
| Shu, 221 | 200,000 | 900,000 | 4.50 |
| Wu, 242 | 520,000 | 2,300,000 | 4.42 |
| Shu at its surrender, 263 | 280,000 | 940,000 | 3.36 |
| Wei, 263, without Shu | 663,423 | 4,432,881 | 6.68 |
| Jin, 280, after taking Wu | 2,459,840 | 16,163,863 | 6.57 |

Du You adds Wei's 4,432,881 persons to Shu's 940,000 and Wu's 2,300,000 and gets 7,672,881
for the three states together, "to support three rulers" (以奉三主). From these numbers:

- Wei held **58%** of the registered persons of the three states (4,432,881 of 7,672,881).
  After it took Shu in 263, the north held **70%** (5,372,881 of 7,672,881).
- The three states together registered **14%** of the persons of 157. Du You's own phrase for
  the years around 200 is "not one or two in ten remained" (十無一二).
- Jin registered 16,163,863 persons in 280, 2.1 times the three-state total, seventeen years
  after Shu's surrender. Growth from the one to the other would take 4.5% a year, compounded,
  for seventeen years.

The last line already says what kind of numbers these are. No pre-modern population doubled in
seventeen years; registration did. The registers counted households that paid tax and served,
not people, and a state that was recovering control of its land added names faster than births
could. The persons per household say the same thing from another side: 3.36 in Shu at its
surrender and 6.68 in Wei in the same year are too far apart to be two populations living the
same way, and more likely reflect what each state's clerks counted. Even the same register is
quoted differently: Du You gives Wu in 242 as 520,000 households and 2,300,000 persons, while
the *Book of Jin*'s treatise on geography (vol. 14) gives 523,000 and 2,400,000. Shu's surrender
list also counted 102,000 soldiers and 40,000 officials against 940,000 persons, one soldier
for every 9.2 registered persons, and Wu's register at its surrender in 280 counted 230,000
soldiers against 2,300,000 persons, one in 10. Ratios that high suggest the register missed many of the people the soldiers came from.

So the registers can't give the size of the states, but they can give their ratio, as long as
each state undercounted in roughly the same way. That is an assumption, and the gap in persons
per household is a reason to hold the 58% loosely. The direction, though, is not in doubt:
every register in the table puts the north well ahead.

## What Guandu decided

Guandu decided who would hold the north, and with it the plain whose registers, even in 263,
outnumbered the other two states' together. It did not decide it easily, and the sources disagree about how close it
was.

Chen Shou's annals of Cao Cao (vol. 1) make Guandu a victory of the few. Cao's force at one point
was "under ten thousand, and two or three in ten wounded" (公兵不滿萬，傷者十二三), against
Yuan Shao's "more than a hundred thousand" (紹衆十餘萬). Pei Songzhi doesn't believe it. His
note gives four arguments: Cao matched Yuan's camps across tens of *li*, which a tiny army
couldn't; Yuan never managed to cut Cao's supply, so he wasn't ten times stronger; the sources
say Cao buried seventy or eighty thousand of Yuan's men, and eight thousand couldn't have bound
them; and Zhong Yao's biography has him send Cao more than two thousand horses, while the
annals give Cao only six hundred-odd riders. Pei's verdict is that "those who wrote it down
wanted to show wonder by making the numbers small, and it is not a true record"
(將記述者欲以少見奇，非其實錄也). A summary that repeats "Cao won with a tenth of Yuan's men"
is repeating the version Pei already rejected.

What the sources do agree on is that Cao nearly gave up. The annals say his grain ran short
and he wrote to Xun Yu "proposing to go back to Xu" (時公糧少，與荀彧書，議欲還許). Xun Yu's
biography (vol. 10) keeps the reply: "Our food is short, but not as short as Chu and Han between
Xingyang and Chenggao. Then neither Liu nor Xiang would be the first to pull back, because the
first to pull back loses" (今軍食雖少，未若楚、漢在滎陽、成皐間也。是時劉、項莫肯先退，先退者勢屈也).
Cao stayed, a defector told him where Yuan's grain was, and the raid on Wuchao ended the
campaign. A victory that turned on a letter and a defector was not structurally certain on the
day. Its consequences were large because of what the north already had: the registers, and the
farm colonies (屯田) Cao had set up around Xu in 196. Pei quotes the *Book of Wei* (魏書), by
Wang Chen, on them: the first year brought in "a million *hu* of grain", and afterwards Cao could
"campaign in every direction without the labour of hauling grain"
(征伐四方，無運糧之勞). After Guandu he needed seven more years to finish Yuan's sons, and in
201 Xun Yu talked him out of turning south against Liu Biao before Hebei was secure.

The Guandu view in its strongest form is Kim Un-hoe's: after Guandu, China was "in effect
unified" (사실상 통일), with only Yizhou, Jingzhou and part of Jiangdong outside Cao's hands
(Kim, 2004). That is true of the land Cao held and false of the outcome, since the remaining
three regions held out for another 63 years (Shu) and 80 (Wu). What Guandu bought was the larger column in the table.

## What the southern strategists conceded

The best evidence that Guandu made the north the favourite comes from the south. Two plans
written within seven years of it, by the two men who shaped Wu's and Shu's strategy, both
start from the north's advantage.

Lu Su (biography, vol. 54) gave his plan to Sun Quan soon after Sun Ce's death in 200: "The house of
Han cannot be restored, and Cao Cao cannot be removed quickly. The only plan for you is to
stand as one leg of a tripod in Jiangdong and watch for a crack in the realm"
(漢室不可復興，曹操不可卒除。為將軍計，惟有鼎足江東，以觀天下之釁). Zhuge Liang's plan
at Longzhong (biography of Zhuge Liang, vol. 35), dated by tradition to 207, says it more sharply. Cao against Yuan
had "a smaller name and fewer men", and won, "not by the timing of Heaven alone but by human
planning"; "now Cao already has a million men and holds the Son of Heaven to command the
lords, and truly cannot be met head-on" (此誠不可與爭鋒).

Zhuge Liang's plan is the usual basis of the view that Shu and Wu had a chance, and read
closely it promises less than that. Its offensive is conditional: hold both Jingzhou and Yizhou,
keep good relations with Sun Quan, govern well, and "when the realm changes" (天下有變) send a
general from Jingzhou toward Wan and Luoyang while Liu Bei leads the army of Yizhou out through
Qinchuan. Both southern plans assume the north will win a straight contest, and both wait for
an event outside their control. Lu Su's goes further and puts the south's real ambition after a
long wait: first take Huang Zu and Liu Biao, "hold the whole length of the Yangzi"
(竟長江所極，據而有之), and only then proclaim an emperor.

## What Chibi showed

Chibi is the battle the novel made famous, and the one the sources agree on least. Chen Shou's
annals of Cao Cao give it one line: "Cao reached Chibi and fought Liu Bei without advantage.
Then a great plague came, and many officers and men died, so he withdrew"
(公至赤壁，與備戰，不利。於是大疫，吏士多死者，乃引軍還). Zhou Yu's biography (vol. 54) quotes a
letter Cao later wrote to Sun Quan: at Chibi "there happened to be sickness"; he burned his own
ships and withdrew, and so "let Zhou Yu falsely take the name for it"
(赤壁之役，值有疾病，孤燒船自退，橫使周瑜虛獲此名). Pei Songzhi's note on the annals quotes the
*Records of the Duke of Shanyang* (山陽公載記), in which Liu Bei burns Cao's ships and Cao's army escapes on
foot through the mud of the Huarong road. Then Pei adds Sun Sheng's remark that the Wu part of Chen Shou's own history (吳志) and this
source put Chibi and Sun Quan's attack on Hefei in opposite orders, and that Chen Shou's Wu
part is right. On the cause, the order of events and who set the fire, the
sources disagree.

Two of the three accounts, the annals and Cao's own letter, put sickness first, and the theme
that the south itself defeated northern armies recurs. In 225 Cao Pi brought more than a
hundred thousand men to Guangling to cross the river, found it too cold for his boats to
enter, and, in a passage Zhang Bo's *Records of Wu* (吳錄)[^zhangbo] preserves in Pei's commentary on Sun Quan
(vol. 47),
looked at the waves and said "Ah! Heaven surely made this to divide south from north!"
(嗟乎！固天所以隔南北也！) and turned back. Nam Deok-hyeon writes that after
Chibi Cao had to give up his dream of winning the realm by marching south
(Nam, 2000). That is the right reading of what Chibi proved, and it is a defensive result.
Chibi showed that the north could not yet cross the Yangzi; it did not give the south a way to
cross the Huai or the Qinling going the other way. The south's ability to survive and its
chance to win are two separate things, and the "chance after Chibi" view runs them together.

## One province, claimed twice

The south's only plan for winning, Zhuge Liang's two-pronged march, needed Jingzhou in Shu's
hands while Wu stayed an ally. Wu's own documents show that Wu could not accept that.

Lu Su's plan of 200 already claims "the whole length of the Yangzi". In 210 Zhou Yu proposed to
Sun Quan that they "advance and take Shu" (進取蜀), join Ma Chao, and then press Cao from
Xiangyang (biography of Zhou Yu, vol. 54). Later Lü Meng advised against an attack north on Xu province, which
open land made hard to hold, and for the alternative: "better to take Guan Yu and hold the whole
Yangzi; our position would be far stronger" (不如取羽，全據長江，形勢益張) (biography of Lü Meng, vol. 54). The
geography is why. Jingzhou lies upstream of Wu's heartland, so whoever holds it can float an
army down onto Jianye. The Longzhong plan's "hold both Jingzhou and Yizhou" (跨有荊、益) and
Wu's "hold the whole Yangzi" claim the same province.

Historians who doubt the Longzhong plan land on this point. Tian Yuqing (田余庆), re-examining
the plan's basis and source, concluded that holding both Jingzhou and Yizhou "lacked
feasibility" (缺乏可行性), in Yan Buke's summary of the essay (Yan, 2015). Zhang Shouzhe
argues from the map that Wu would always seize Jingzhou, so that Guan Yu's loss of it and Liu
Bei's defeat at Yiling were "the necessary result of this geographic arrangement"
(这种地缘安排的必然结果) (Zhang, 2017). The opposing reading is Huang Pumin and Sun Jianmin's,
who call the plan a sound strategic blueprint whose two-route march was only over-idealised;
they also quote Mao Zedong's marginal note, which put the error in splitting the army across a
thousand *li* (Huang and Sun, 2019). Even the defenders concede the march.

## 219: the moment the plan waited for, and its end

For a few months in 219 the Longzhong plan's conditions were closer to met than at any other
time. That year Liu Bei took Hanzhong, killing Cao Cao's general Xiahou Yuan, and named himself
King of Hanzhong, so Shu held Yizhou and the gate north through the mountains, while Guan Yu
held Jingzhou. Guan Yu's biography (vol. 36) gives the rest of the
year, the Xiangyang–Fancheng campaign (襄樊之戰, 양번 전투), in a few lines:

> 是歲，羽率眾攻曹仁於樊。曹公遣于禁助仁。秋，大霖雨，漢水汎溢，禁所督七軍皆沒。禁降羽，羽又斬將軍龐德。梁、郟、陸渾群盜或遙受羽印號，為之支黨，羽威震華夏。曹公議徙許都以避其銳。
>
> That year Guan Yu led his army against Cao Ren at Fan. Lord Cao sent Yu Jin to help Cao Ren.
> In autumn the rain went on for days, the Han overflowed, and the seven armies under Yu Jin
> were all drowned. Yu Jin surrendered to Guan Yu, and Guan Yu beheaded the general Pang De.
> Bandits in Liang, Jia and Luhun took Guan Yu's seals and titles from afar and became his
> partisans, and his might shook the central lands. Lord Cao discussed moving the capital away
> from Xu to avoid his edge.

Yu Jin's own biography (vol. 17) adds that the water stood "several *zhang* deep on level
ground" (平地水數丈), and that Guan Yu came at the stranded army in large boats. Liang, Jia and
Luhun are counties south of Luoyang, well inside Wei. A Shu general had destroyed a field army,
raised revolts in the north's own territory, and made Cao Cao think of moving his court. If the
realm ever "changed" in the sense Zhuge Liang meant (天下有變), it was in the autumn of 219.

The next sentence of the biography is the north's answer, and it is not an army:

> 司馬宣王、蔣濟以為關羽得志，孫權必不原也。可遣人勸權躡其後，許割江南以封權，則樊圍自解。曹公從之。
>
> Sima Yi and Jiang Ji held that if Guan Yu got what he wanted, Sun Quan would surely not want
> it. Send someone to urge Sun Quan to follow at his heels, promise to cut off the land south
> of the Yangzi as his fief, and the siege of Fan would lift by itself. Lord Cao agreed.

Wei's counsellors saw the flaw in the Longzhong plan as clearly as Wu's strategists did: they
did not need to beat Guan Yu, only to let Wu do it. And the moment found the plan with one hand.
Its offensive was a march from Jingzhou toward Wan and Luoyang *together with* an army out of
Yizhou; in 219 only Jingzhou moved, while Liu Bei's army, just back from Hanzhong, stayed in the
west.

Lü Meng's biography (vol. 54) shows how Guan Yu's victory itself set off
the attack on him. Lü Meng had feigned illness so that Guan Yu would strip the garrisons he
had left behind against Wu, and Guan Yu did. Then "Guan Yu took Yu Jin and all his men, tens
of thousands of men and horses; pleading a shortage of grain, he seized the rice at Xiang pass
without leave. When Sun Quan heard of it, he set out" (羽盡擒禁等，人馬數萬，託以糧乏，擅取湘關米。權聞之，遂行).
The prisoners from the flood had to be fed, and the grain Guan Yu took to feed them was Wu's.
Lü Meng hid his best soldiers in covered boats rowed by men in plain white clothes dressed as merchants,
seized Guan Yu's river posts so that no warning reached him, and received the surrender of
Shi Ren at Gong'an and Mi Fang at Jiangling. Xu Huang's relief force meanwhile broke the siege
of Fan (biography of Zhang Liao, vol. 17). Guan Yu's biography ends the year: "Sun Quan already held Jiangling and had captured
the wives and children of all Guan Yu's officers and men, so his army scattered. Sun Quan sent
generals to meet him, and they beheaded Guan Yu and his son Ping at Linju"
(權已據江陵，盡虜羽士眾妻子，羽軍遂散。權遣將逆擊羽，斬羽及子平於臨沮).

Chen Shou places one more fact between Sima Yi's advice and Wu's attack: Sun Quan had earlier
asked for Guan Yu's daughter as a wife for his son, and Guan Yu had cursed the envoy and
refused, "and Sun Quan was furious" (權遣使為子索羽女，羽罵辱其使，不許婚，權大怒). Placed
there it reads like a cause, and the novel makes Guan Yu's pride the reason Jingzhou fell. Wu's
own papers say otherwise. Lü Meng's "take Guan Yu and hold the whole Yangzi" was advice given
before the campaign, Lu Su's claim on the whole river went back to 200, and Zhou Yu had wanted
Shu itself in 210. The insult may have set the date; the claim on the province did not depend
on it.

The view that 219, not 222, was the real turning point is common. Qin Shiyong writes that "it
was Guan Yu's defeat and Jingzhou's change of hands that finally fixed the three-state pattern
of the late Eastern Han" (正是關羽覆敗、荊州易主，最終奠定了東漢末年的三國格局), and that
"the one that profited most from Wu's seizure of Jingzhou was Wei" (東吳襲荊州，大獲其利的是曹魏集團)
(Qin, n.d.). Both halves fit the sources. Sima Yi's advice was precisely a plan for Wei to
profit from a fight between its two enemies, and it worked: the siege of Fan lifted, Wei lost
no land, and the alliance that the Longzhong plan needed was broken by the ally. The registers
add a qualification. Jingzhou passed from one southern state to the other, so nothing in 219
touched the north's share of the counted population. What 219 fixed was the shape of the south,
not the size of the north.

Liu Bei's march down the Yangzi in 221 was the attempt to take Jingzhou back. At Yiling, Lu Xun
let the two armies face each other for seven or eight months, then set fire to its camps, broke
more than forty of them, and sent Liu Bei fleeing to Baidi; "its boats, weapons and stores, by
water and land, were nearly all lost at once" (其舟船器械，水步軍資，一時略盡) (biography of
Lu Xun, vol. 58).

So the south's chance, as its own plan defined it, did not end at Yiling. It ended in 219 when
Jingzhou changed hands, and it was flawed from the start, because the plan needed an ally whose
own strategy required the same province. Fancheng was the nearest the plan came to its moment,
and the same campaign showed why the moment could not be used. Yiling was the failed appeal
against 219. After it, Zhuge Liang's campaigns of 228 onward went north through the mountains
from Hanzhong alone, the one prong of the two that was left.

## What made the north's victory near certain

If Guandu made the north the favourite and Chibi showed that the favourite could not cross the
river, the remaining question is what changed that. The answer the sources give is the upper
Yangzi.

Wei took Shu in 263, and the registers moved from 58% to 70%. The larger change was the
river. The *Book of Jin*'s biography of Wang Jun (vol. 42) describes the fleet Jin built
in Yizhou for the attack on Wu: linked ships 120 paces square holding more than two thousand
men, with wooden walls and towers. Wood shavings from the yards floated down past the Wu
garrison, and its commander Wu Yan warned Sun Hao that "if Jianping does not fall, they will
never dare to cross" (建平不下，終不敢渡). In 280 Du Yu told the generals at Jiangling that if
Wang Jun took Jianping he would "ride the current straight down" (順流長驅). He did, and Sun
Hao surrendered to Wang Jun at Shitou. The river that stopped Cao Cao and Cao Pi from the north
was crossed from the west, by a fleet built in the province Wei gained in 263.

This is the same geography as the Jingzhou quarrel, seen from the other end. Whoever holds the
upper river can descend on the lower, and Wu spent its alliance to secure Jingzhou against Shu,
only to face the upstream threat again once Shu fell to the north.

## Who counts the dates

How the question is posed also depends on whose history is read. Chen Shou, a former Shu
official writing under Jin, gave Wei the imperial annals and Liu Bei only a biography. The *Book
of Jin* records that his father had been punished under Zhuge Liang and that Chen Shou judged
Zhuge Liang's generalship "not his strength" (將略非長), which some held against him
(biography of Chen Shou, vol. 82). In the 4th century Xi Zuochi wrote the *Annals of Han and Jin* (漢晉春秋) to rebuke
Huan Wen's ambitions; it treated Shu as the legitimate Han, Wei as usurpers, and dated the end
of Han and the start of Jin to the fall of Shu (至文帝平蜀，乃為漢亡而晉始興焉)
(biography of Xi Zuochi, vol. 82). The *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, which shapes how most readers in Korea and
China meet the period, follows the Shu-centred line, and the view that the south "still had a
chance" after Chibi is partly that line's sympathy.

Xi Zuochi's date and the strategic one coincide. For him 263 was when Heaven's mandate passed;
here it is when the upper river did. Neither is the year of Guandu or of Yiling.

## Summary

The debate between Guandu and Yiling is a debate between two questions. Guandu (200) settled
who would hold the north, and with it the larger share of every register in the table: 58% of
the counted persons as late as 263. It was a near thing on the day, and the version in which
Cao won with a tenth of Yuan Shao's army is the one Pei Songzhi already rejected. Its effect
shows in the southern plans of 200 and 207, which both concede that the north cannot be beaten
head-on. Chibi (208) showed that the north could not yet take the south; it did not give the
south a way to win. The south's one plan for winning needed Jingzhou to be Shu's while Wu
remained a friend, Wu's plans needed Jingzhou too, and that chance ended when Lü Meng took
Jingzhou in 219, in the same campaign in which Guan Yu had come closest to the plan's moment at
Fancheng; Wei won it by letting Wu strike, without losing ground. Yiling (222) was the failed attempt to undo it. The north's victory became
close to certain in 263, when Wei took Shu and the upper Yangzi, and Jin's fleet came down that
river in 280. The registers can rank the states but not size them, and the persons per
household differ too much between states to trust the shares to better than a rough ratio.
