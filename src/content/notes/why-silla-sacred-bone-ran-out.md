Silla ranked its people by "bone" (骨, 골) and "head rank" (頭品, 두품). At the top were two bones: the
sacred bone (聖骨, 성골) and the true bone (眞骨, 진골). The usual account gives the same
ending: the sacred bone died out with Queen Jindeok in 654, and Kim Chunchu became the first
true-bone king, King Muyeol. Two questions hide in that sentence. Was the sacred bone a real
category in the seventh century, or a label applied afterwards? And if it was real, was it
bound to give way, or did it end by accident? This note takes a position on both from the
sources: the full scheme is a later construction, the rule underneath it was real, and the
rule was fragile by design but ended when it did partly by chance.

The sources are few and late. Kim Busik's *Samguk sagi* (三國史記, 삼국사기, "History of the
Three Kingdoms", 1145) and the monk Iryeon's *Samguk yusa* (三國遺事, 삼국유사, "Memorabilia of
the Three Kingdoms", late 13th century) were both written in Goryeo, five and six centuries
after the events. The *New Book of Tang* (新唐書, 1060) keeps a Tang envoy's description of
Silla from the 760s. Korean scholarship is the main literature, and much of it is known here
from encyclopedia articles and journal abstracts; where that is so, the text says whose summary
it is. Quotations are given in the original with a translation; translations are our own. These works
are divided into volumes (卷, 권), each literally a "scroll" and closer to a long chapter than
to a modern book, and are cited by volume number ("vol. 5").

Throughout, the note returns to one family: King Jinheung's descendants over three
generations. Every person in the argument is on this tree.

Jinheung's eldest son Dongnyun (銅輪, 동륜) was made crown prince and died in 572, before his father.
The throne went to the second son, Jinji, whose names are given as Saryun (舍輪, 사륜) or Geumnyun
(金輪, 금륜). After Jinji came Dongnyun's son Jinpyeong, who reigned 53 years. Jinpyeong had no son.
His elder daughter Deokman became Queen Seondeok; his brother Gukban's daughter Seungman became
Queen Jindeok. His other daughter, Cheonmyeong, married Jinji's son Yongchun, and their son
was Kim Chunchu (*Samguk sagi*, vols. 4 and 5).

Two facts on the tree carry most of the argument. Chunchu was Jinpyeong's grandson, his
closest male descendant, and a great-grandson of Jinheung through both parents. And yet he was
true bone, while Jinpyeong's daughter and his niece were sacred.

## What the two histories say

Kim Busik gives the division once, at the end of Jindeok's annals (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 5,
Jindeok's 8th year):

> 國人謂始祖赫居世至眞德二十八王，謂之聖骨；自武烈至末王，謂之眞骨。唐令狐澄新羅記曰：其國王族，謂之第一骨，餘貴族第二骨。
>
> The people of the country call the twenty-eight kings from the founder Hyeokgeose to Jindeok
> the sacred bone, and those from Muyeol to the last king the true bone. The *Record of Silla*
> of Linghu Cheng of Tang says: the royal clan of that country is called the first bone, and
> the other nobles the second bone.

Read closely, the passage claims less than it seems to. Kim Busik doesn't say Silla had a
sacred bone; he says "the people of the country call" (國人謂) the kings that, a report of
usage. The twenty-eight kings he lists include the Park and Seok kings who ruled before the Kim
house took the throne for good, so "sacred bone" here can't mean a kin group of the Kim royal
family. It is a periodization of the whole dynasty into two halves, which is how a later age
would sort a king list. And the Chinese quotation he adds talks of a first and second bone,
not of sacred and true; placing it there invites the reader to equate them, a step the
quotation itself doesn't take.

Iryeon draws the line differently. His chronological table (王曆, 왕력) divides Silla into an "early
antiquity" (上古, 상고) that ends with King Jijeung, a "middle antiquity" (中古, 중고) from Beopheung to
Jindeok, and a "late antiquity" (下古, 하고) from Muyeol on. At Jindeok's entry he notes 已上中古聖骨,
"the above, middle antiquity, sacred bone", and the kings after her are true bone. So Iryeon's
sacred bone is six kings, Beopheung to Jindeok, not twenty-eight. At Seondeok's entry he gives
the reason she reigned:

> 聖骨男盡，故女王立
>
> The men of the sacred bone had run out, so a woman was made king.

Kim Busik says nothing of the kind. His account of her accession is plain: "King Jinpyeong
died without a son, and the people of the country set up Deokman" (眞平王薨，無子，國人立德曼)
(*Samguk sagi*, vol. 5). The best-known sentence about the sacred bone, that its men ran out,
comes from a 13th-century table, not from the annals.

The two histories also disagree about Jinji, the king whose line matters most. Kim Busik's
annals end his reign with "the king died" (王薨) in his fourth year (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 4). Iryeon,
in the tale of the woman Dohwa (桃花女, 도화녀), says that he ruled four years, "his government was
in disorder and he was dissolute, and the people of the country deposed him"
(政亂荒婬，國人廢之) (*Samguk yusa*, vol. 1). Iryeon's version explains why Jinji's grandson
would be excluded; Kim Busik's leaves it a puzzle.

The two accounts are evidence of different kinds. Kim Busik's twenty-eight-king version is a
later periodization and should not be read as a seventh-century category. Iryeon's narrower
version fits the seventh-century record much better, as the next section shows, though it too
is a retrospective table.

## What Silla did

If the label is late, the behaviour it labels is not. Three things the sources record from the
time make sense only if eligibility for the throne was restricted to Dongnyun's line.

The first is the names. Jinpyeong's personal name was Baekjeong (白淨, 백정) and his queen was Lady
Maya (摩耶夫人, 마야부인); in his first year he made his younger brothers Baekban (伯飯, 백반) and Gukban (國飯, 국반)
royal "*galmunwang*" (葛文王, 갈문왕) (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 4). These are the names of the Buddha's family:
Śuddhodana, the Buddha's father, is 白淨 or 淨飯 in Chinese; Māyā is his mother; and
Śuklodana and Droṇodana (白飯, 斛飯) are Śuddhodana's brothers. Choe Byeong-heon reads the set as
a claim that Jinpyeong's house was the Buddha's own clan, the second stage of a royal
self-sanctification that began under Jinheung with the image of the wheel-turning king
(轉輪聖王, 전륜성왕) (Choe, 2021). Jinheung's sons' names, "bronze wheel" and "golden wheel", are wheels
of that king.

The second is a claim made on the house's behalf by a monk. When Jajang was in China, the
bodhisattva Mañjuśrī told him, in Iryeon's account of the nine-storey pagoda at Hwangnyongsa
(*Samguk yusa*, vol. 3):

> 汝國王是天竺剎利種。王預受佛記。故別有因緣。
>
> Your country's king is of the kṣatriya stock of India. The king has received the Buddha's
> prophecy in advance, and so has a special bond of cause and condition.

Jajang returned in 643, so "your king" is Queen Seondeok. The story is in a late collection,
but it says what Jinpyeong's names say: the ruling line was of the Buddha's caste, and set
apart from other people for that reason. Seondeok's own title, recorded in the *New Book of
Tang* (vol. 220) as well as by Kim Busik, was "Holy Ancestor, Imperial Lady"
(聖祖皇姑, 성조황고), with the same character 聖 that the later "sacred bone" uses.

The third, and the strongest, is the two queens. Silla had never had a woman ruler. In 632 it
crowned Deokman, and in 647 Seungman, while adult men of royal blood were alive: Chunchu was
born in 604 (NIKH, "김춘추와 김유신") and was 28 when Seondeok took the throne. A kingdom that would rather crown its
first and then its second woman than a king's grandson was not choosing on nearness of blood.
It was choosing by a rule that Chunchu failed and the two women passed. The rule also cost
something: in 647 the highest noble, Bidam (毗曇, 비담), made *sangdaedeung* (上大等, 상대등) two years
before, rose in revolt saying "a woman ruler cannot govern well" (女主不能善理) (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 5). A rule that a kingdom kept at the price of a rebellion is hard to explain as a later
historian's invention.

What the rule was can be read off the tree. Every sacred-bone ruler after 579 descends from
Dongnyun through his sons. Chunchu descends from Dongnyun too, but through a daughter,
Cheonmyeong, and his father's line runs back to the deposed Jinji. So the rule was not "both
parents royal", which Chunchu met, nor nearness to the last king, which Chunchu met better than
Jindeok did. It was membership in Dongnyun's male line. Membership could be held by women,
but it passed only through men.

## What the first bone was

The one outside description of Silla's bones is the Tang envoy's. Gu Yin (顧愔) went to Silla
as deputy envoy after King Gyeongdeok's death in 765, for the investiture of the boy king
Hyegong (*New Book of Tang*, vol. 220). The NIKH essay identifies him as the author of a *Record of
the Kingdom of Silla* (新羅國記), which Linghu Cheng quoted, so Kim Busik's quotation goes back
to it. The *New Book of Tang* keeps the fuller passage:

> 其建官，以親屬爲上，其族名第一骨、第二骨以自別。兄弟女、姑、姨、從姊妹，皆聘爲妻。王族爲第一骨，妻亦其族，生子皆爲第一骨，不娶第二骨女，雖娶，常爲妾媵。
>
> In making appointments they put kin first. The clans are called first bone and second bone,
> to set themselves apart. Brothers' daughters, father's sisters, mother's sisters and
> cousins are all taken as wives. The royal clan is the first bone; their wives are of that
> clan too, and the sons they have are all first bone. They do not marry women of the second
> bone, and if they do, it is always as concubines.

Two readings of this passage are in the Korean literature, and the essays of the National
Institute of Korean History's own reader take opposite sides. One reads the first bone as the
sacred bone and the second as the true bone, while noting that earlier research made the first
bone the true bone (NIKH, "신라 성골과 진골"). The other argues that a sacred bone in the 760s
is impossible, since Seondeok reigned because its men had run out, and so both bones were true
bone, with a ranking inside it (NIKH, "신라의 골품제").

The date settles it for the second reading. Gu Yin described Silla a century after both
histories end the sacred bone, under kings both call true bone. What he describes, then, is the
true-bone royal house of the eighth century, and it was already endogamous: it married nieces,
aunts and cousins, and children of a first-bone mother and father stayed first bone. That
matters for the theory that the sacred bone was defined by marriage, with both parents of the
royal house. If true-bone kings married the same way, close endogamy can't be what set the
sacred bone apart. Nor does the passage support the sacred bone at all; it shows only that a
bone was inherited through both parents in the royal house a century later.

## Was the end bound to come?

A rule that passes membership only through sons, and admits no one from outside, makes a
category that can only shrink unless its men have sons. That is the sense in which the sacred
bone was "doomed", and it is worth testing how strong the sense is.

In Jinpyeong's generation the line rested on three men: Jinpyeong and his brothers Baekban and
Gukban. No son of any of them appears in the sources. Suppose, as a simple model and not as a
fact about Silla, that the number of sons a man leaves who live to adulthood follows a Poisson
distribution with mean $m$. A population that just replaces itself has $m = 1$. The chance
that one man leaves no such son is $e^{-m}$, and that three men all fail is $e^{-3m}$. The
long-run chance that a male line dies out, from the theory of branching processes, is the
smallest root $q$ of $q = e^{m(q-1)}$, and for three founders it is $q^3$.

*The same model run forward. Each curve is the chance that a male line founded by three men has
died out by a given generation; the dots are the first generation, the event that happened in
Silla. At replacement (1 son each) the curve keeps climbing, but slowly: after twelve
generations, some three centuries, the line has still survived about one time in three. With
more sons than replacement it levels off below 0.1.*

The table and figure cut both ways. A line that only replaces itself dies out with certainty in
the long run, however many founders it starts with, so a sacred bone defined as a male line was fragile
by construction. But the failure of all three brothers in a single generation was unlikely: 1
chance in 20 at replacement, less if a king with several consorts had more sons than the
average man. Jinpyeong married twice; Iryeon's table names a later queen, Lady Seungman
(僧滿夫人, 승만부인), besides Maya. On this model, the end in 632 was bad luck, not arithmetic.

What made the end certain was the rule's other half. Once only women were left, the line could
not be carried on. Seondeok and Jindeok were members, but their children would have belonged
to their husbands' lines, as Cheonmyeong's son Chunchu did. Seondeok passed the throne to her cousin, and Jindeok died "without an heir" (無嗣). So
"doomed" is right from 632, when the last man died, and too strong for 579, when the line had
three men in it. Two women's reigns bought 22 years, and the rule could have been changed at
any point to admit a daughter's son. That it wasn't, through a revolt and a second queen, is
the best measure of how much the rule meant to the people who kept it.

## How the true bone came to the throne

The true bone was not a new class that rose against the sacred. It was the rest of the same
family, already ranked and already in office. In 650, under Jindeok, an order directed that
"those of true bone who hold office carry ivory tablets" (以眞骨在位者，執牙笏) (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 5). When Jindeok died in 654, the question was only which true-bone man would reign.

Kim Busik tells it twice, in two different ways. The annals of Muyeol (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 5) have
the ministers ask Alcheon (閼川, 알천), *sangdaedeung* since 647, to act as regent. Alcheon refuses, calling
himself old and without virtue to speak of (臣老矣，無德行可稱), and names another: "for virtue
and repute today none is the equal of Lord Chunchu, truly a hero to save the age"
(今之德望崇重，莫若春秋公，實可謂濟世英傑矣).
Chunchu declines three times and takes the throne only because he must. Kim Yusin's biography
in the same book (*Samguk sagi*, vol. 42) says it in one line:

> 眞德大王薨，無嗣，庾信與宰相閼川伊飡謀，迎春秋伊飡，卽位。
>
> Queen Jindeok died without an heir. Yusin plotted with the minister Alcheon, *ichan*, to
> bring in Chunchu, *ichan*, and he took the throne.

The annals give the ceremony of a man chosen for his virtue, the biography gives a decision by
two powerful men. Iryeon's story of the nobles' council at Ujiam under Jindeok adds the
balance of power between them: Alcheon was strong enough to kill a tiger by its tail and sat at
the head of the meeting, "yet all the lords submitted to Yusin's authority"
(然諸公皆服庾信之威) (*Samguk yusa*, vol. 1). Chunchu had been the envoy who won the Tang
alliance in 648, and Yusin held the army. Blood made Chunchu eligible once the rule was gone;
the alliance with Yusin made him king.

## Where historians disagree

The literature agrees on almost nothing about the sacred bone except that it ended with
Jindeok. The *Encyclopedia of Korean Culture* states that most researchers accept that much
(성골 article, 1995, revised 2023). Beyond it, four positions recur.

**It was not real, or not separate.** Early Japanese scholars, in the NIKH essay's summary,
doubted that the sacred bone existed or took it to be the same as the true bone. Choe Byeong-heon
quotes Imanishi Ryū (今西龍) as suggesting the change came from Jinji's deposition or Yongchun's
mother's line while doubting the sacred bone existed at all. Choe's own view goes further: the
sacred bone "was not a status that really existed apart from the true bone"
(진골과 구분되는 별개의 신분으로 실재하는 것이 아니었다), but a political rhetoric of the
Yongchun–Chunchu group, a Buddhist myth to raise the weakened authority of the two queens (Choe,
2021). The encyclopedia lists further views that the label was applied afterwards, under Muyeol
or Munmu, or as late as Queen Jinseong in the late ninth century.

**It was a branch that set itself apart.** Yi Gi-dong, in Choe's summary, explained the sacred
bone by lineage division (分枝化, 분지화): Jinpyeong's branch rose above the rest of the royal kin.
Yi's own encyclopedia article describes it as the highest status, held by those of the Kim
royal house qualified to become king, extinct with Jindeok, and says that the reason for the
distinction "is not clearly known". The encyclopedia's sacred-bone article gives a version tied
to Jinpyeong: his line made Dongnyun the main line and claimed the sacred bone to set itself
apart from Jinji's.

**It began earlier, under Jinheung.** Seong Jun-seon argues that Jinheung, drawing on the
wheel-turning king, called his own blood group the sacred bone, with the right to succeed as its
privilege, and prepared Geumnyun (Jinji) to succeed after Dongnyun died (Seong, 2021). This fits
Iryeon's table, which counts Jinji as sacred bone, and needs Jinji's deposition to explain why
his grandson was not.

**It came back.** The main later source is the stele of the monk Muyeom (無染, 무염; Nanghye 朗慧, 낭혜;
d. 888), written by Choe Chiwon. Kim Du-jin's encyclopedia article on the true bone notes that
Muyeom, a descendant of King Muyeol, was of head rank six although his father Beomcheong (範淸, 범청)
had been true bone, so a family could lose a bone. A small note in the inscription explains the ranks, and
Park Su-jin's review of the field reads its gloss as 聖而(曰)眞骨, with three interpretations:
"a sacred true bone", a division between sacred and true bone, or a revival of the sacred bone
in the late ninth century, as fact or as idea (Park, 2021). The inscription itself was not
checked character by character for this note, since no reliable copy could be opened.

This note's position sits between the first two. Choe's reading, that the label served the
queens and the men around them, explains why the word is late and why it carries the Buddha's
clan; and none of the seventh-century passages quoted here uses it. But the restriction it
names was real, because the kingdom behaved by it: it crowned two women over a king's grandson
and fought a revolt to keep doing so. What would count against this reading is a son of
Baekban or Gukban, alive in 632 and passed over for Seondeok. The sources name none.

## Summary

Kim Busik's sacred bone, twenty-eight kings from the founder to Jindeok, is a later
periodization, reported as what "the people of the country call" the kings, and the Tang
description he sets beside it is of the eighth-century true-bone house, not of a sacred bone.
Iryeon's narrower sacred bone, six kings from Beopheung to Jindeok, fits the seventh-century
record, and behind it was a real rule: from 579 the throne stayed in the male line of
Jinheung's son Dongnyun. The rule shows in the Buddha-clan names of Jinpyeong's family, in
the monk's claim that the ruler was of the kṣatriya caste, and above all in the crowning of two
women over Kim Chunchu, Jinpyeong's grandson through a daughter and the deposed Jinji's
grandson through his father. A male line that admits no one is fragile, and dies out for
certain if it merely replaces itself; but three brothers all failing to leave a son was a 1 in
20 chance at replacement, so the timing was bad luck. From 632, with only women left, the end
was certain. The true bone that followed was the excluded half of the same family, and
Chunchu reached the throne in 654 through the backing of Kim Yusin and the minister Alcheon.
Whether anyone called the rule "sacred bone" while it was in force is not shown by the sources
quoted here, and the leading readings of the one later inscription disagree.
