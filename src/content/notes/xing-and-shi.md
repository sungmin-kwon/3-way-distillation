The Chinese surname, which Korea and Vietnam adopted, was once two names. Before the third century BCE an aristocrat could carry a xing (姓, xìng), the name of birth, and a shi (氏, shì), the name of grant. This note reads the primary texts that define the two, shows what each was for, and argues that their division of labour explains both who bore which name and why they could merge.

The sources are the *Zuo Zhuan* (左傳, "Zuo's Commentary", traditionally attributed to Zuo Qiuming 左丘明), the *Guoyu* (國語, "Discourses of the States"), the *Book of Rites* (禮記, *Liji*), the *Book of Documents* (尚書, *Shangshu*) and the *Classic of Poetry* (詩經, *Shijing*), with two later readers of them: Zheng Qiao (鄭樵) in the twelfth century and Gu Yanwu (顧炎武) in the seventeenth. Quotations are given in the original with translations; translations are our own. The *Zuo Zhuan* is cited by the Lu ruler's reign and year (隱公八年, Duke Yin 8), the *Guoyu* by state and chapter ("Jin" 4, 晉語四), the classics by chapter. The later works are divided into volumes (卷), literally scrolls, closer to a long chapter than to a modern book, and are cited by volume.

## One passage, two rules

The sharpest statement of the difference sits in the *Zuo Zhuan*, Duke Yin 8. The Lu minister Wuhai (無駭) has died, Yu Fu (羽父) asks the duke for a posthumous name and a lineage name for him, and the duke asks Zhong Zhong (眾仲), who answers:

> 天子建德，因生以賜姓，胙之土而命之氏。諸侯以字為諡，因以為族。官有世功，則有官族，邑亦如之。
>
> When the Son of Heaven establishes the virtuous, he bestows a xing according to their birth, and grants them land and with it names their shi. Among the feudal lords, the courtesy name serves as the posthumous name, and from it comes the lineage. Where an office has been held with merit for generations, there is a lineage named for the office; a fief works the same way.

The record then closes: 公命以字為展氏, "the duke ordered that the courtesy name be made the Zhan shi". Zhan (展) was the courtesy name of Wuhai's grandfather.

Read sentence by sentence, the passage separates the two names by their source. The xing is 因生, "according to birth": the king confirms it, but what he confirms is descent. The shi is 胙之土而命之氏, named together with a grant of land, and the rest of the answer lists the other ways a lineage got one: from an ancestor's courtesy name (字), from an office (官), from a fief (邑). The case at hand shows the mechanism: a new lineage name was created by decree, for one man's descendants, out of his grandfather's courtesy name. A shi could be made; a xing could only be inherited.

The *Book of Documents* pairs the two from the side of the ruler. At the end of the "Tribute of Yu" chapter (禹貢), after the survey of the land and its tribute, the edition opened for this note reads:

> 咸則三壤成賦。中邦錫土、姓，祗台德先，不距朕行。
>
> All were graded by the three classes of soil, and the tribute was fixed. In the central states land and xing were bestowed: "Let them revere my virtue first, and none resist my conduct."

Other editions punctuate 咸則三壤，成賦中邦。錫土姓, attaching "the central states" to the tribute; either way 錫土、姓 grants land and xing together, the land that a shi would be named for and the xing that marked descent.

## The xing: birth, descent and marriage

The *Guoyu*, "Jin" 4, gives the xing a descent logic, and it makes the point through two brothers. The speaker is Sikong Jizi (司空季子), advising the exiled prince Chong'er of Jin, then in Qin, to accept a marriage he hesitated over:

> 昔少典娶于有蟜氏，生黃帝、炎帝。黃帝以姬水成，炎帝以姜水成。成而異德，故黃帝為姬，炎帝為姜。……異姓則異德，異德則異類。異類雖近，男女相及，以生民也。同姓則同德，同德則同心，同心則同志。
>
> Long ago Shaodian took a wife from the Youjiao clan, and she bore the Yellow Emperor and the Flame Emperor. The Yellow Emperor grew up by the Ji river, the Flame Emperor by the Jiang river. Grown up apart, their 德 (inborn potency) differed, so the Yellow Emperor was Ji and the Flame Emperor was Jiang. … A different xing means a different potency, and a different potency a different kind. Different kinds, however close, may join man and woman, and so people are born. The same xing means the same potency, the same potency the same heart, the same heart the same will.

The detail that matters is that the two emperors have one father and one mother. Their xing differ anyway, because each takes his name from the river he grew up by. So in this account the xing does not simply copy a parent's name: it names a 德, a kind, and marriage is allowed only across kinds. The same speech counts the Yellow Emperor's sons in twenty-five lines, of whom fourteen "obtained a xing", in twelve xing (其得姓者十四人，為十二姓). That shows what kind of thing a xing was: a small, old, closed set of names shared by a whole descent group, not a personal label.

From that logic follows the rule the *Book of Rites* states as an ordinance ("Summary of the Rules of Propriety", part 1, 曲禮上): 取妻不取同姓, "in taking a wife, do not take one of the same xing".[^guoyu]

The sources give two accounts of where a xing comes from, and both are worth holding at once. The *Zuo Zhuan* has the Son of Heaven bestow it, 因生以賜姓, a grant from above that confirms descent; the *Guoyu* derives it from a kind of potency, fixed by where an ancestor grew up. Neither derives it from the mother. Later scholarship has read the xing variously as a matrilineal clan name, a totem, or a political grant; those readings are not checked against their texts here.[^origins]

The function explains the form. A name that marks the group within which marriage is forbidden must be shared by everyone born into the group, and it must travel with a woman when she marries out, because the prohibition is checked by xing. That is why Zheng Qiao, in the preface to the "Treatise on Clans and Lineages" of his *Comprehensive Treatises* (通志, 1161, vol. 25), could sum the system up as 男子稱氏，婦人稱姓: men are called by their shi, women by their xing.[^formula] The usage is visible in the texts themselves. In the *Zuo Zhuan* a wife is named by her natal xing: 衛莊公娶于齊東宮得臣之妹，曰莊姜 (Duke Yin 3), "Duke Zhuang of Wei married the younger sister of Dechen, the heir of Qi, called Zhuang Jiang": Zhuang from her husband, Jiang the xing of the house of Qi. The *Classic of Poetry* poem on her ("The Tall Lady", 衛風·碩人) places her the same way, by father and husband: 齊侯之子，衛侯之妻, "daughter of the lord of Qi, wife of the lord of Wei". Men, by contrast, are called by their shi: Zhao Dun (趙盾) of the Zhao lineage of Jin, Fan Xuanzi (范宣子) of the Fan, though both belonged to other xing.

## The shi: land, office and rank

The shi did the opposite job. Because it was granted with land or an office, or created by decree as Zhan was, it existed only where the feudal order existed, and only for those the order had granted something to: aristocratic men. A lineage could change its shi when it gained a new fief or office, and a man could be known by more than one: Fan Xuanzi's lineage was also called Shi (士), after an office an ancestor had held.

That is the link between the two systems. The xing organized marriage across descent groups; the shi marked rank and property inside the aristocracy; and the rule that men are named by shi and women by xing follows from the first. A woman married out of her natal group, so the name that identified that group, the xing, was the one she kept, while the granted name belonged to the men who held the grants. The two names were not two words for one thing. They were two institutions with different jobs.

## Why the two names merged

Zheng Qiao's account of the end is that the distinction lasted through the Three Dynasties and was lost after the Qin conquest of 221 BCE, when the old ruling houses lost their fiefs and their descendants became commoners.[^merger] Gu Yanwu, reading the *Records of the Grand Historian* (史記, *Shiji*, c. 94 BCE) five centuries later, located the fusion in a historian's usage rather than an event (*Record of Daily Knowledge*, 日知錄, vol. 23, "Clans and lineages", 氏族):

> 姓氏之稱自太史公始混而為一，本紀於秦始皇則曰姓趙氏，於漢髙祖則曰姓劉氏
>
> The terms xing and shi were first confused into one by the Grand Historian. In the annals he says of the First Emperor of Qin, "his xing was the Zhao shi", and of the founding emperor of Han, "his xing was the Liu shi".

The example is well chosen. The Qin royal house traced itself to the xing Ying (嬴); Zhao was a shi, the name of a fief an ancestor received. Writing 姓趙氏, Sima Qian puts the shi in the slot of the xing. His biography of Confucius does the same, 字仲尼，姓孔氏 (vol. 47), although Kong was a lineage name of Song nobles whose xing was Zi (子).[^shiji]

The two accounts fit together if the cause is the one this note has followed. A shi needed a grant behind it; once fiefs were replaced by commanderies and offices stopped being hereditary, no new shi of the old kind could be made, and the old ones stopped marking rank. A xing needed a closed set of descent groups and a nobility that kept track of them; a commoner like Liu Bang had neither. What survived was the name a family actually used, which was usually the old shi, now inherited by everyone in the male line and called by either word. The marriage rule survived too, transferred to the merged name: the later prohibition on marrying someone of the same surname is the xing rule applied to what had been shi.

## Where the merged name went

Korea and Vietnam took the merged Chinese surname. Korea kept something of the old distinction in the 본관 (本貫, bon'gwan, "origin seat"), the place a clan claims as its origin: 김해 김씨 (金海金氏, Gimhae Gim) and 경주 김씨 (慶州金氏, Gyeongju Gim) share the surname but are different clans, and the old Korean ban on marriage within the same surname and seat (동성동본, 同姓同本) applied the marriage rule to the pair. Korean and Vietnamese women also traditionally keep their own surname at marriage, as a woman of Qi stayed Jiang in Wei.[^eastasia]

Japan borrowed the two characters but not the institution. In the Yamato state 氏 (uji) named a clan, such as the Soga (蘇我) or the Mononobe (物部), and 姓 (kabane) named a hereditary rank title held by the clan, such as omi (臣) or muraji (連): the second character changed its job. Later the court lineage names such as Minamoto (源) or Fujiwara (藤原) were carried beside a 苗字 (myōji) taken from an estate, a second two-name system, until the Meiji government made a surname compulsory for every commoner in 1875.[^eastasia]

## Where the evidence is thin

The texts that define the two names are later than the society they describe. The *Zuo Zhuan* and the *Guoyu* were compiled in the Warring States period, centuries after the events they narrate, and the speeches in them, Zhong Zhong's and Sikong Jizi's included, are reconstructions that may say more about how fourth-century writers understood names than about how eighth-century aristocrats used them. The "Tribute of Yu" is set in the age of the sage Yu but is generally dated much later, so its 錫土、姓 is an idea of kingship, not a record of grants. The Sikong Jizi speech is also an argument with a purpose: it explains the marriage rule in order to argue that a particular marriage is allowed.

The clean formula is the latest evidence of all. 男子稱氏，婦人稱姓 is Zheng Qiao's twelfth-century summary, not a line from a pre-Qin text, and it compresses usage that the *Zuo Zhuan* shows to be untidier: a woman's name there combines her xing with whatever places her, her birth order, her husband's posthumous name or her home state, and a man's shi could change with his fief. The two later readers also disagree on the cause and the date of the merger. Zheng Qiao ties it to the fall of the feudal houses under Qin; Gu Yanwu finds it in Sima Qian's vocabulary, a century later. The argument above, that the merger followed from the end of hereditary grants, is consistent with both but is an inference from the institutions, not a statement in either source. Bronze inscriptions of the Western Zhou, which name women by xing in the period itself, would be the contemporary test, and none were read for this note.

## Summary

Before the Qin, Chinese aristocrats ran two naming institutions. The xing came with birth, was shared by a descent group and forbade marriage within it; the *Zuo Zhuan* has the king confirm it, the *Guoyu* derives it from an ancestor's kind of potency, and neither from the mother. The shi was granted with a fief or an office or made from an ancestor's courtesy name, marked rank, and was borne by aristocratic men. Because marriage was checked by xing, women were named by their xing and men by their shi. When hereditary grants ended, the shi stopped marking rank and the xing stopped being tracked, and the name families actually used was inherited as one surname; by Sima Qian's time the two words named it interchangeably, as Gu Yanwu noticed. Korea and Vietnam adopted that merged surname; Japan used the two characters for a different pair of names.
