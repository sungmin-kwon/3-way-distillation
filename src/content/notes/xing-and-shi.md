Every East Asian family name, from a Korean 성 to a Vietnamese họ to a Japanese surname, goes back to one Chinese institution. Before the third century BCE that institution ran on two names, not one: the xing (姓, xìng), the name of birth, and the shi (氏, shì), the name of grant. This note reads the primary texts that define the two, shows what each was for, and argues why they eventually merged into a single hereditary surname.

The sources are the *Zuo Zhuan* (左傳, "Zuo's Commentary", traditionally attributed to Zuo Qiuming 左丘明), the *Guoyu* (國語, "Discourses of the States"), the *Liji* (禮記, "Book of Rites"), the *Shangshu* (尚書, "Book of Documents") and the *Shijing* (詩經, "Classic of Poetry"). Quotations are given in the original with translations; translations are our own. Citations give the section as the received editions divide the texts: the *Zuo Zhuan* by the Lu ruler's reign and year (隱公八年, Duke Yin 8), the *Guoyu* by state and chapter (晉語四, "Jin" 4), the *Liji*, *Shangshu* and *Shijing* by chapter.

## One passage, two rules

The sharpest statement of the difference sits in the *Zuo Zhuan*, Duke Yin 8, where 羽父 (Yu Fu) asks the ruler for a posthumous title and a lineage name for 無駭 (Wuhai):

> 天子建德，因生以賜姓，胙之土而命之氏。諸侯以字為諡，因以為族。官有世功，則有官族。邑亦如之。
>
> The Son of Heaven establishes the worthy: according to birth he bestows a xing, and with an allotment of land he appoints a shi. For the feudal lords, the courtesy name serves as posthumous title, and from it comes their lineage. Where an office has hereditary merit, there is an "office lineage"; a fief works the same way.

Read sentence by sentence, the passage separates the two names by their source. The xing is 因生, "according to birth": it is not chosen but given at birth and inherited with it. The shi is 胙之土而命之氏, "appointed together with a grant of land", and the same breath names the other grants it could come from: a posthumous title (諡), an office (官), a fief (邑). The *Shangshu* pairs the two from the other side. In the "Tribute of Yu" chapter (禹貢), the survey of the land ends:

> 咸則三壤，成賦中邦。錫土姓，祗台德先，不距朕行。
>
> The soils were all graded into three classes and the tribute of the central kingdom was settled. Land and xing were bestowed; all revered his virtue and none resisted his rule.

錫土姓, "bestowed land and xing", grants the two together: soil for the shi, surname for the xing.

## The xing: birth, descent and marriage

Because the xing was inherited by birth and marked the group one was born into, the sources give it a descent logic. The *Guoyu*, "Jin" 4, derives the two oldest xing from the mothers' clans:

> 昔少典娶於有蟜氏，生黃帝、炎帝。黃帝以姬水成，炎帝以姜水成。成而異德，故黃帝為姬，炎帝為姜。... 異姓則異德，異德則異類。異類雖近，男女相反，以生民也。同姓則同德，同德則同心，同心則同志。
>
> In the past Shao Dian married a woman of the Youjiao clan and begat the Yellow Emperor and the Flame Emperor. The Yellow Emperor was formed by the Ji waters, the Flame Emperor by the Jiang waters. Formed differently, their 德 (inborn potency) differed, so the Yellow Emperor was Ji and the Flame Emperor was Jiang. ... Different xing means different potency, different potency means a different kind. Different kinds, though close, join male and female and beget people. The same xing means the same potency, the same potency the same heart, the same heart the same will.

The same chapter counts the Yellow Emperor's twenty-five sons, of whom fourteen "received a xing" in twelve surnames (黃帝之子二十五人，其得姓者十有四人，為十二姓). That shows what kind of thing a xing was: a small, old, closed set of names shared by a whole descent group, not a personal label. The chapter draws the marriage rule from the same logic, 同姓不婚, "do not marry within the same xing", and the *Liji* states the rule as a practical ordinance (曲禮上): "取妻不取同姓" ("in taking a wife, do not take one of the same xing").

The sources themselves give two accounts of where the xing comes from, and both are worth holding at once. The *Zuo Zhuan* has the Son of Heaven bestow it, 因生以賜姓, a grant from above; the *Guoyu* derives it from the mother's clan and river, a descent from below. Later scholarship has read the xing variously as a matrilineal clan name, a totem, or a political grant; those readings are not checked against their texts here.[^origins]

The function explains the form. A name that exists to mark the group within which marriage is forbidden must be shared by everyone born into the group, and it must travel with a woman when she marries out, because the prohibition is checked by xing. That is why the received formula, stated in later ritual and genealogical literature, is 男子稱氏，女子稱姓: men are named by their shi, women by their xing.[^formula] The usage is visible in the texts themselves. In the *Zuo Zhuan* a woman is identified through her father's xing: "衛莊公取於齊東宮得臣之妹，曰莊姜" (隱公三年), "Duke Zhuang of Wei took as wife the younger sister of Dechen of the Eastern Palace of Qi and called her Zhuang Jiang." In the *Shijing*, the poem on her (衛風·碩人) calls her "齊侯之子，衛侯之妻", "the daughter of the lord of Qi, the wife of the lord of Wei." Men are called by their shi: 士會 of the Shi clan of Jin, 趙盾 of the Zhao, 范宣子 of the Fan.

## The shi: land, office and rank

The shi did the opposite job. Because it was granted with land, an office or a title, it existed only where the feudal order existed, and only for those the order had granted something to: aristocratic men. The *Zuo Zhuan* passage above lists its sources, fief, office, posthumous title, and a man could hold more than one, one shi from his fief and another from his office. Because it marked rank, losing the grant meant losing the name.

That is the link between the two systems. The xing organized marriage across descent groups; the shi marked rank inside the aristocracy; and the rule that men are named by shi and women by xing follows from the first. A woman married out of her natal group, so the name that identified her natal group, the xing, was the name she kept, while the acquired name, the shi, belonged to the men who held the grants. The two names were not two words for one thing. They were two institutions with different jobs.

## Why the two names merged

The shi stood on the feudal order. When Qin destroyed the six states in 221 BCE, the standard account continues, it reduced their descendants to commoners (子孫為庶民), and with the grants of land, office and title went the names they generated; what remained was the xing, now used as a hereditary surname for everyone.[^merger] By the Han the two words were written as one: Sima Qian describes Confucius as "字仲尼，姓孔氏" (孔子世家), "courtesy name Zhongni, xing Kong, shi Kong", the fused form in which surname and clan name already count as a single name.[^shiji]

The merged name is what went abroad. Korea took the single hereditary surname (성, 姓) and added the 本貫 (bon'gwan, "origin seat", the place a clan claims), as in 金海金氏 (Gimhae Gim). Vietnam took the Chinese surname (họ). Japan kept the old uji (氏) names, such as 蘇我氏 (Soga) or 物部氏 (Mononobe), longest, added the myōji (苗字) beside them, and only required every household to bear a surname in the Meiji period, in the 1870s.[^eastasia]

## Summary

Before the Qin, China ran two naming institutions. The xing was given by birth, shared by a descent group and used to forbid marriage within it, 同姓不婚; the sources derive it from the mother's clan (*Guoyu*) or from a grant by the Son of Heaven (*Zuo Zhuan*), and this note holds both readings. The shi was acquired from a fief, an office or a posthumous title, marked rank, and was borne by aristocratic men; hence men by shi, women by xing. When the feudal order collapsed after the Qin conquest, the shi lost its base, the two names merged into one hereditary surname, and Han writers already used them as one. That merged surname is the ancestor of the family names of China, Korea, Vietnam and Japan. Where this note leans on later scholarship or on general knowledge rather than on a text opened for it, the footnotes say so.
